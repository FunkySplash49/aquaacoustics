================================================================================
 PART 1   THE PROBLEM AND THE CORE IDEA
================================================================================

THE PROBLEM
-----------
Water utilities lose a very large fraction of what they pump to leaks in buried
pipes. The pipe is underground; you cannot see the leak. Digging to look is
expensive, so you need to know WHERE to dig before you start - ideally to within
a metre or two over hundreds of metres of pipe.

THE PHYSICAL INSIGHT
--------------------
A leak in a pressurised pipe is not silent. Fluid escaping through a small hole
is turbulent, and turbulence radiates sound: a continuous broadband hiss. That
sound travels along the pipe wall and the water column in both directions.

So: clamp one acoustic sensor at each end of a pipe section. Both sensors hear
THE SAME hiss. But not at the same instant - sound takes time to travel, so the
sensor nearer the leak hears it FIRST.

    Sensor A                    leak                        Sensor B
      |==========================X============================|
      0 m                       63 m                       200 m
      <------- 63 m ------------>|<-------- 137 m ---------->

    Sound reaches A after  63 m / 1200 m/s = 0.0525 s
    Sound reaches B after 137 m / 1200 m/s = 0.1142 s
    Difference                             = -0.0617 s

That difference is called the TIME DIFFERENCE OF ARRIVAL, or TDoA. If you can
measure it, simple geometry gives you the leak position. Measuring it is the
entire technical problem, and it is harder than it sounds: the difference is
tens of milliseconds, buried in noise, between two signals that look like
featureless hiss.

THE CORE IDEA IN ONE SENTENCE
-----------------------------
Both sensors record the same noise at different times; cross-correlating the
two recordings reveals the time offset at which they best line up, and that
offset converts directly into a distance.

WHY CROSS-CORRELATION IS THE RIGHT TOOL
---------------------------------------
The two recordings each contain:

  (a) the leak signal - SHARED between the channels, just time-shifted
  (b) sensor noise    - INDEPENDENT in each channel

Cross-correlation asks "how well do these two signals agree if I slide one by
t?" The shared component reinforces itself at the correct t. The independent
noise does not reinforce at any t, because it has nothing to agree with. So the
correlation naturally amplifies the thing you want and suppresses the thing you
do not.

That independence is the whole reason this works, and it is why the simulator
gives each sensor its own separate noise draw.

 PART 2   THE SYSTEM AT A GLANCE
================================================================================

THREE STAGES
------------

  STAGE 1  ACQUIRE    Synthesise (or, on real hardware, read) the two
                      sensor recordings.
                      -> src/aeld/signals.py

  STAGE 2  LOCALISE   Measure the TDoA with GCC-PHAT.
                      -> src/aeld/gccphat.py

  STAGE 3  CONVERT    Turn the TDoA into metres from Sensor A.
                      -> src/aeld/gccphat.py (locate_leak)

  Orchestration and timing of all three: src/aeld/detector.py
  User interface:                         app.py

  (There used to be a Stage 2 CNN filter between acquisition and localisation.
  It is archived - see 4.4.)


DATA FLOW
---------

    SimulationConfig            <- built from the sliders on page 1
           |
           v
    signals.generate_sensor_signals(leak_position, config)
           |    band-limited noise -> fractional delay -> attenuate -> +noise
           v
    sensor_a, sensor_b          <- THE ONLY THING DOWNSTREAM STAGES SEE
           |
           +---> gccphat.gcc_phat()              Stage 2: measure tau
                        |
                        v
                 gccphat.locate_leak(tau)        Stage 3: tau -> metres
                        |
                        v
                 DetectionResult -> st.session_state -> page 2 plots


THE INFORMATION BOUNDARY  (important, and worth saying out loud in a demo)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Only Stage 1 ever knows the true leak position - it has to, because it is
simulating the physics. Stages 2 and 3 receive NOTHING but the two waveform
arrays, exactly as they would from real hardware.

The true position is carried through the pipeline only so the result can be
SCORED afterwards. It never influences the answer. This matters: it is what
makes the accuracy figures meaningful rather than circular.

 PART 4   HOW EACH STAGE WORKS
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4.1  STAGE 1 - ACQUISITION (the forward model)      src/aeld/signals.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This stage answers: "if a leak were at position d, what exactly would the two
sensors record?" It is the physics simulation. Four steps.

STEP 1 - Build the leak's acoustic signature
--------------------------------------------
  white noise  ->  Butterworth bandpass (100-2000 Hz)  ->  normalise

  Why white noise: a turbulent jet radiates broadband sound with no clean
  tones. White noise has flat power at every frequency, which is exactly that.

  Why band-limit it: real leak energy is concentrated in the low kHz. A 4th
  order Butterworth bandpass shapes flat noise into that realistic profile.

  ONE CRITICAL DETAIL: the filter is applied with scipy's `sosfiltfilt`, which
  runs the filter forwards and then backwards. This gives ZERO PHASE
  DISTORTION. A normal one-pass filter delays the signal slightly, and any
  delay it introduced would be indistinguishable from the travel-time delay we
  are trying to measure - it would inject a fake reading straight into the
  quantity of interest. `sosfiltfilt` eliminates that risk entirely.

  Also: `sos` (second-order sections) rather than transfer-function form,
  because SOS is far more numerically stable at 4th order and above.

STEP 2 - Delay each copy by its travel time
-------------------------------------------
  time to A = distance_A / v          time to B = distance_B / v

  These are converted to samples (time x fs) and applied.

  THE SUBTLE PART - why the delay must be FRACTIONAL:
  A leak can sit anywhere along the pipe, so the true delay is almost never a
  whole number of samples. If you rounded to the nearest sample, every answer
  would be quantised: at 8 kHz and 1200 m/s, one sample of delay equals 0.15 m
  of path difference, so rounding builds in an error floor for no reason.

  The fix uses the Fourier shift theorem. A pure time shift is a linear phase
  ramp in the frequency domain:

        x(t - T)   <->   X(f) . exp(-2.pi.i.f.T)

  So: FFT the signal, multiply by that complex exponential, inverse FFT. The
  exponent is continuous in T, so fractional delays cost nothing extra.

  TWO GOTCHAS, both handled in the code:

    (a) The FFT treats the signal as periodic, so this shift is CIRCULAR -
        samples pushed off the end wrap around to the front as garbage. The
        code generates a longer source with guard padding at both ends,
        applies the shift, then cuts a clean window out of the MIDDLE.

    (b) For an even-length real signal, the final FFT bin is Nyquist. It has
        no conjugate partner, so an inverse real FFT REQUIRES it to be real.
        The phase ramp makes it complex for any fractional delay, and the
        inverse FFT then silently discards the imaginary part - distorting the
        waveform and leaking energy. The code zeroes that bin, which is the
        standard treatment. (This was a real bug caught by the tests; it was
        costing ~0.5% of signal energy before the fix.)

STEP 3 - Attenuate by distance
------------------------------
  amplitude factor = exp(-alpha x distance),  alpha = 0.004 /m by default

  Sound loses energy as it propagates. This is why the nearer sensor's trace
  is visibly taller in the UI - a useful visual cue, though the algorithm does
  not rely on amplitude at all.

STEP 4 - Add independent sensor noise
-------------------------------------
  Each channel gets its OWN Gaussian noise draw, scaled by the noise slider.

  The independence is the point, not an implementation detail. Shared signal +
  independent noise is the structure cross-correlation exploits. If both
  channels received the SAME noise, the method would break down completely,
  because the noise would correlate just as well as the signal.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4.2  STAGE 2 - GCC-PHAT, THE ACTUAL MEASUREMENT      src/aeld/gccphat.py
       This is the section to explain if you only explain one.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE STARTING POINT: why plain cross-correlation is not good enough
------------------------------------------------------------------
Ordinary cross-correlation would find the delay, but it has two weaknesses:

  1. Its peak is only as sharp as the source's own autocorrelation. Our leak
     noise is band-limited, so plain correlation produces a broad, rounded
     hump. A broad peak means an imprecise delay - you cannot tell exactly
     where the top is.

  2. Loud frequencies dominate. A low-frequency rumble contributes to the
     answer in proportion to how LOUD it is, not how INFORMATIVE it is.

THE FIX: throw away magnitude, keep only phase
----------------------------------------------
GCC-PHAT stands for Generalized Cross-Correlation with PHAse Transform. The
whole algorithm is four lines of maths:

  1. Transform both signals:      X_A(f) = FFT(sensor_a)
                                  X_B(f) = FFT(sensor_b)

  2. Cross-power spectrum:        R(f) = X_A(f) . conj(X_B(f))

  3. PHAT whitening:              R'(f) = R(f) / |R(f)|

  4. Back to the lag domain:      r(t) = IFFT( R'(f) )

     Then: tau = the lag t at which r(t) peaks.

WHY STEP 3 IS THE CLEVER BIT
----------------------------
Suppose sensor A's signal really is sensor B's, delayed by d. Then:

        X_A(f) = X_B(f) . exp(-2.pi.i.f.d)

Substitute into the cross-power spectrum:

        R(f) = X_A . conj(X_B)
             = X_B . exp(-2.pi.i.f.d) . conj(X_B)
             = |X_B(f)|^2 . exp(-2.pi.i.f.d)
               ^^^^^^^^^^^   ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
               magnitude     the delay information

Now divide by the magnitude:

        R'(f) = exp(-2.pi.i.f.d)

That is a PURE PHASE RAMP - and the inverse Fourier transform of a pure phase
ramp is a PERFECT SPIKE at lag d.

The insight: the magnitude was never carrying the timing information. Only the
phase was. Discarding magnitude therefore loses nothing you needed, and gains
two things: every frequency now contributes equally (so no loud band can
dominate), and the peak collapses from a broad hump to a sharp spike.

WHAT THE CODE ACTUALLY DOES, STEP BY STEP
-----------------------------------------
  1. Zero-pad to length len(A)+len(B). The FFT correlation theorem gives
     CIRCULAR correlation; padding makes the circular result equal the linear
     one, so energy from one end cannot masquerade as correlation at the other.

  2. rfft both signals (real-input FFT: half the bins, same information).

  3. Multiply A by the conjugate of B -> cross-power spectrum.

  4. Divide by |.| + eps -> PHAT whitening. (eps prevents division by zero.)

  4b. BAND-LIMIT: zero every bin outside 100-2000 Hz. See PART 5 - this is
      the single biggest robustness win in the whole system.

  5. Inverse FFT at 4x length -> the correlation, on a 4x finer lag grid.

  6. Reorder so lag zero sits in the middle, giving a plottable axis running
     from -max_shift to +max_shift. Optionally restrict to physically
     reachable lags only (|tau| can never exceed L/v).

  7. argmax to find the peak. Deliberately on the SIGNED correlation, not its
     absolute value: the true delay produces a POSITIVE spike, and using abs()
     could latch onto a negative trough and would also break step 8.

  8. Parabolic interpolation for sub-sample precision (see PART 5).

  9. Compute sharpness = peak height / mean level -> the confidence metric.

THE SIGN CONVENTION (getting this wrong mirrors every answer)
------------------------------------------------------------
  POSITIVE tau  =>  sensor A's signal arrived LATER  =>  leak nearer B
  NEGATIVE tau  =>  sensor A's signal arrived EARLIER =>  leak nearer A

There is a dedicated test (`test_sign_convention`) pinning this down, because
a sign error would place every leak at the wrong end of the pipe while looking
entirely plausible.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4.3  STAGE 3 - FROM TIME DELAY TO POSITION       gccphat.locate_leak()
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Pure geometry, no signal processing. Let d = distance from Sensor A,
L = pipe length, v = wave velocity.

        time to A  =  d / v
        time to B  =  (L - d) / v

        tau  =  time to A - time to B
             =  d/v - (L-d)/v
             =  (2d - L) / v

Rearranging for d:

        v . tau  =  2d - L
        2d       =  L + v . tau

        ****************************
        *   d  =  (L + v.tau) / 2  *
        ****************************

CHECK IT AT THE THREE POINTS YOU KNOW
-------------------------------------
  Leak at the midpoint:  tau = 0      ->  d = L/2          correct
  Leak at Sensor A:      tau = -L/v   ->  d = (L-L)/2 = 0   correct
  Leak at Sensor B:      tau = +L/v   ->  d = (L+L)/2 = L   correct

All three are unit tests. The result is finally clipped to [0, L], because
noise can push the estimate slightly past an endpoint and "a leak 3 m beyond
the end of the pipe" is not a physical answer.

NOTE THE SENSITIVITY: position scales directly with v. A 5% error in the
assumed wave velocity produces a 5% error in position. In the field, v is the
parameter you most need to calibrate.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4.4  THE REMOVED CNN STAGE, AND WHY IT WENT      archive/ml_model/
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT WAS THERE
--------------
An earlier version of this pipeline had a Stage 2 between acquisition and
localisation: a PyTorch convolutional neural network that was supposed to
answer "is this acoustic event even a leak?" before the localiser spent effort
on it. The motivation is real - a deployed sensor triggers constantly on
passing trucks, pumps starting, valves closing and ordinary water use, and
localising every one of those would bury operators in false alarms.

WHY IT WAS REMOVED
------------------
The network was never trained. Its weights were random, and the code computed a
genuine output and then deliberately ignored it, returning a hard-coded 0.97
instead. It was a placeholder, exactly as originally scoped.

Kept in the pipeline, that placeholder cost:

  * THE HEAVIEST DEPENDENCY. PyTorch is a very large install, and the only
    thing in the project that needed a platform-specific wheel.
  * THE SLOWEST STAGE. 73.4 ms of the 75.9 ms total, against 0.36 ms for the
    real localisation - almost entirely one-off Metal GPU context setup.
  * A MISLEADING NUMBER IN THE UI. "97% leak probability" looked like a
    measurement and was not one. Worse, it competed for attention with the
    peak-sharpness figure, which is the number that actually determines whether
    a result can be trusted.

Removing it made the system 33x faster end to end (75.9 ms -> 2.3 ms), dropped
the PyTorch dependency entirely, and made the interface honest by default
rather than honest-with-a-caveat.

NOTHING WAS DELETED
-------------------
archive/ml_model/cnn_filter.py is the complete original module, unmodified.
archive/ml_model/README.md documents:

  * what is genuinely real in it (the network, the spectrogram feature, the
    real forward pass, MPS/CUDA/CPU device selection with a CPU fallback, and
    the fixed 64x64 input resampling that works around a genuine MPS
    limitation on non-divisible adaptive pooling)
  * what is not (the untrained weights and the hard-coded probability)
  * exactly how to reinstate it - file restore, __init__ re-export, the
    detector.py stage insertion, the five DetectionResult fields to restore,
    and `python setup.py --with-ml`
  * a four-point checklist for training it into something real

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE SYSTEM AS IT STANDS
-------------------------------------------
There is now NO machine learning in the active pipeline. That is worth stating
plainly rather than hiding, because it is a strength for this particular job:
the localisation is deterministic, explainable line by line, and covered by
tests that would fail if the maths broke. Nothing about the reported position
depends on a learned model.

The honest cost is a real capability gap: the system assumes what it hears is a
leak. It cannot tell a leak from a passing truck. Closing that gap needs a
trained classifier, which needs labelled field recordings - and that is the
single largest remaining step towards a product.

================================================================================
 PART 5   THE THREE REFINEMENTS THAT MAKE IT ACCURATE
================================================================================

Textbook GCC-PHAT gets you a delay. These three additions are what take it
from "roughly right" to sub-millimetre in the worked example.

REFINEMENT 1 - PARABOLIC SUB-SAMPLE INTERPOLATION
-------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM: the correlation is only evaluated at discrete lags, but the true peak
almost always lies BETWEEN two of them. Taking the highest sample caps your
accuracy at half a sample - here, 0.075 m of position.

SOLUTION: fit a parabola through the highest sample and its two neighbours,
then solve for the vertex:

        offset = 0.5 . (y_prev - y_next) / (y_prev - 2.y_peak + y_next)

That offset (roughly -0.5 to +0.5) is added to the integer peak index. Three
lines of arithmetic for an order-of-magnitude accuracy gain.

The denominator is the discrete second derivative - the curvature. If it is
near zero the three points are collinear, there is no well-defined vertex, and
dividing would explode, so the code bails out with no correction. It also
rejects any fitted offset beyond one full sample as a pathological fit.

REFINEMENT 2 - SPECTRAL UPSAMPLING  (interp = 4)
------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM: refinement 1 assumes the peak is parabola-shaped. It is not. After
whitening, the ideal peak is SINC-shaped (a Dirichlet kernel). Fitting a
parabola to a sinc across whole samples has a SYSTEMATIC bias, worst when the
true peak sits near halfway between two samples.

This was measured, not theorised: a known 33.25-sample delay came back as
33.14 - an error of 0.11 samples, which exceeded the accuracy target.

SOLUTION: zero-pad the spectrum before the inverse FFT. Zero-padding in the
frequency domain is EXACT sinc interpolation in the time domain - it evaluates
the same continuous correlation function on a 4x finer lag grid. The parabola
then only has to fit a much narrower slice of the sinc, where a parabola is a
good local approximation. Residual bias falls by roughly interp^2.

Effect in the worked example: lag resolution improves from 0.000125 s to
0.00003125 s, i.e. from 0.075 m to 0.01875 m of position per lag step - before
interpolation is even applied.

REFINEMENT 3 - BAND-LIMITING THE CORRELATION  (the big one)
-----------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM: this is the flip side of PHAT's strength. Whitening forces every
frequency bin to unit magnitude. That is what sharpens the peak - but it also
means a bin containing NOTHING BUT NOISE is amplified to exactly the same
weight as a bin full of clean leak signal. Its phase is random, so it votes
for a random lag.

Our leak radiates only 100-2000 Hz. Sensor noise is broadband, across the full
0-4000 Hz. So above 2 kHz there is NO SIGNAL AT ALL - yet unrestricted PHAT
gives those bins full voting rights. At low SNR they outvote the real ones and
the estimate collapses.

SOLUTION: zero every bin outside the leak's band, after whitening. In the
worked example this leaves 1901 of 4001 bins contributing - the other 2100
were pure noise and are now silent.

MEASURED EFFECT: in a heavy-noise test case, error fell from 136 m to 41 m -
a 3.3x improvement from a handful of lines.

The trade-off, stated honestly: a narrower band means a broader sinc, so the
peak widens slightly and precision drops a little. That is a small price for a
large robustness gain, and it is the right trade.


================================================================================
 PART 6   WHY THERE IS A CONFIDENCE GATE (the most important part)
================================================================================

THE DISCOVERY
-------------
Accuracy was measured across noise levels, 60 random seeds each, on a 200 m
pipe at 1200 m/s:

    noise level | median error | within 2 m | mean sharpness
    ------------|--------------|------------|----------------
       0.00     |     0.00 m   |    100%    |     69.7
       0.05     |     0.00 m   |    100%    |     61.1
       0.20     |     0.00 m   |    100%    |     27.3
       0.50     |     0.01 m   |     97%    |      8.4
       1.00     |    65.35 m   |     13%    |      4.6
       2.00     |    64.21 m   |      2%    |      4.6

Read that table carefully. It is not a gentle slope - it is a CLIFF.

Up to noise 0.5 the system is essentially exact. At 1.0 the median error is
65 m on a 200 m pipe, which is barely better than guessing. There is almost no
middle ground.

WHY IT FAILS THAT WAY
---------------------
Past a certain noise level GCC-PHAT does not DRIFT - it JUMPS. The correlation
peak stops being the tallest feature, some noise artefact elsewhere wins the
argmax, and the reported lag becomes essentially random. The root cause is the
one described in refinement 3: whitening gives noise-dominated bins the same
vote as signal-dominated ones.

WHY THIS IS DANGEROUS, NOT JUST INACCURATE
------------------------------------------
The system still returns a number. It looks just as precise as a good one -
"the leak is at 112.6 m", to four decimal places. Acting on it means sending a
crew to dig up a road in the wrong place.

A confidently wrong answer is worse than no answer at all. This is the central
safety problem in the system, and it needs an explicit guard.

THE GUARD
---------
Alongside the delay, GCC-PHAT reports

        sharpness = peak height / mean correlation level

which measures how far the peak stands clear of the noise floor. A tall,
isolated spike means a real measurement. A peak barely above the floor means
the argmax was arbitrary. Crucially, this is computed WITHOUT any knowledge of
the true answer, so it is exactly what a real deployment would have available.

`DetectionResult.is_confident` compares it against a threshold, and the UI
switches from a green located position to an explicit "location unreliable".

HOW THE THRESHOLD WAS CALIBRATED - INCLUDING GETTING IT WRONG FIRST
-------------------------------------------------------------------
This part is worth telling honestly, because it is a real lesson.

  FIRST ATTEMPT: 320 runs suggested a threshold of 6.0 gave 100% precision -
  every estimate above it was accurate, and the worst inaccurate one scored
  5.81. Clean separation. It looked settled.

  IT WAS WRONG. Broader sweeping found a counterexample: sharpness 6.25 with a
  26.6 m error. A CONFIDENTLY WRONG result - exactly the failure the gate
  exists to prevent. The threshold had been overfitted to too narrow a sample.

  RECALIBRATION: 1400 runs spanning noise 0.0-2.0, four pipe geometries
  (100-500 m, 1000-1500 m/s) and random leak positions. The worst inaccurate
  estimate scored 7.98.

  FINAL THRESHOLD: 10.0 - about 25% margin above the worst observed failure,
  while still accepting 62% of all runs.

    threshold | confident runs | precision | recall
    ----------|----------------|-----------|-------
        6     |   611 / 1400   |   98.04%  |  84.0%
        7     |   544 / 1400   |   99.82%  |  76.2%
        8     |   509 / 1400   |  100.00%  |  71.4%
       10     |   445 / 1400   |  100.00%  |  62.4%   <- chosen
       15     |   342 / 1400   |  100.00%  |  48.0%

  Note that 8.0 also achieves 100% precision, but with ZERO margin - it sits
  exactly at the worst observed failure. 10.0 buys headroom.

THE HONEST CAVEAT
-----------------
This is an EMPIRICAL bound from one simulator, not a proof. It says "across
1400 synthetic runs, nothing above 10.0 was wrong." It does not guarantee
nothing ever will be. A real deployment must re-derive the threshold from
field data.

THE TEST THAT ENFORCES IT
-------------------------
`test_never_confidently_wrong` is the most important test in the suite. It
sweeps 105 runs across noise 0.0-2.0 and varying leak positions, and asserts
the actual safety invariant:

        WHENEVER the system declares itself confident, it MUST be accurate.

Being inaccurate is permitted - but only when also flagged unreliable. The
test also guards against passing trivially by never being confident at all
(it requires at least 25 confident results).

This is a much more valuable property than any arbitrary error tolerance,
because it is the property an operator actually depends on.

 PART 9   SUGGESTED DEMO SCRIPT

A five-minute run-through that builds the idea before showing the result.

  1. FRAME THE PROBLEM  (30 s)
     "The pipe is buried. We need to know where to dig, to within a metre,
     over 200 metres. A leak hisses continuously - so put a microphone at each
     end. Both hear the same hiss, but the nearer one hears it first."

  2. SHOW THE CONFIGURATION  (page 1, 45 s)
     Point out the three sliders. Emphasise wave velocity: "position scales
     directly with this, so it is the number you calibrate in the field."
     Note the derived panel - especially "resolution per sample", which shows
     what one sample of timing is worth in metres.

  3. TRIGGER A RANDOM LEAK  (15 s)
     Press the button. The position is chosen randomly and hidden from the
     detector. Note the reported total time.

  4. SHOW WHAT THE SENSORS HEARD  (page 2, 45 s)
     "Two traces of hiss. One is slightly louder because it is closer. The
     time offset we need is about 500 samples - completely invisible by eye.
     That is why we need mathematics, not inspection."

  5. THE KEY SLIDE - THE CORRELATION CURVE  (90 s)
     "This asks: how well do the two recordings agree if I slide one against
     the other? The peak is the offset where they line up - and that offset IS
     the answer." Show the detected peak line sitting on top of the true-delay
     line. Then tick 'Zoom in on the peak' and explain that the dots are the
     discrete lags computed, the true peak falls between them, and a parabola
     through the top three dots finds the vertex - which is where sub-sample
     precision comes from.

  6. THE ARITHMETIC  (30 s)
     Show d = (L + v.tau)/2 with the real numbers substituted, and the error.
     Then the to-scale pipe diagram: true vs. detected marker.

  7. THE BEST PART - BREAK IT ON PURPOSE  (60 s)
     Go back to page 1, push the noise slider to 1.5, and trigger again.
     The result page turns red: "location unreliable."
     "GCC-PHAT does not fail gracefully - past a certain noise level it stops
     drifting and starts jumping to random lags. It would still hand you a
     number that looks precise to four decimal places. So the system measures
     how far the correlation peak stands above the noise floor, and refuses to
     commit when that margin is too small. The threshold was calibrated over
     1400 runs. A system that knows when it doesn't know is worth more than one
     that is occasionally spectacularly wrong."

  8. IF ASKED ABOUT MACHINE LEARNING  (20 s)
     Be straight: there is none, deliberately. An untrained CNN placeholder was
     removed because it added the heaviest dependency and the slowest stage
     while contributing nothing - the system got 33x faster without it. The
     localisation is deterministic signal processing, explainable line by line
     and covered by tests. The honest gap is that it cannot yet tell a leak
     from a passing truck; that needs a trained classifier and labelled field
     recordings. The module is archived and ready to revive.


================================================================================
 PART 10   QUESTIONS YOU ARE LIKELY TO BE ASKED
================================================================================

Q: Why not just use the loudest sensor to tell you which end it is near?
A: Amplitude depends on pipe material, soil, sensor coupling and the size of
   the leak - all unknown and highly variable. Timing depends only on distance
   and wave speed. Timing is a far more reliable observable, which is why the
   algorithm ignores amplitude entirely.

Q: Why GCC-PHAT rather than plain cross-correlation?
A: Plain correlation gives a broad, rounded peak for band-limited noise, and
   lets loud frequencies dominate regardless of how informative they are.
   Whitening makes every frequency contribute equally and collapses the peak
   to a sharp spike. See PART 4.2.

Q: How accurate is it?
A: At default settings (noise 0.15) the error is around a tenth of a
   millimetre - the localisation is essentially exact when the signal supports
   it. It stays sub-centimetre up to noise 0.5. Past that it fails, and the
   confidence gate says so. Full table in PART 6.

Q: What is the resolution limit?
A: One sample of delay at 8 kHz and 1200 m/s equals 0.075 m of position.
   Spectral upsampling takes that to 0.019 m, and parabolic interpolation goes
   below it again. In practice the limiting factor is not sampling - it is
   uncertainty in the assumed wave velocity.

Q: Does it work on a real pipe?
A: The signal processing would transfer directly. What this prototype does not
   model is multipath from junctions and branches, frequency-dependent
   attenuation, and dispersion. Those complicate the correlation and would be
   the main work in a field version.

Q: How many sensors do you need?
A: Two per pipe section, which gives exactly one solution. Three or more makes
   the problem over-determined, which allows self-consistency checks - a
   genuinely valuable next step.

Q: Is machine learning doing the localisation?
A: No - there is no machine learning in the system at all. The localisation is
   pure signal processing: deterministic, explainable, and unit-tested. An
   untrained CNN placeholder was removed (see PART 4.4); its intended job was
   rejecting non-leak events, not locating them.

Q: Isn't the lack of AI a weakness?
A: Not for this task. Locating the leak is a physics problem with an exact
   closed-form answer, so a learned model would be strictly worse: slower,
   opaque, and impossible to unit-test. Machine learning earns its place on the
   DIFFERENT question of classifying what the sound is - and there it needs
   labelled field data before it means anything.

Q: How would you know if it mistook a truck for a leak?
A: Right now, you would not - and that is the honest capability gap. The
   confidence gate answers "can I trust this location?", never "is this really
   a leak?". Closing it is the top item in PART 12.

Q: What happens if there are two leaks?
A: Not handled. The correlation would show two peaks and the code takes the
   larger. Multi-leak separation is a genuinely harder problem and out of
   scope here.

 PART 11   WHAT IS REAL AND WHAT IS NOT

A single table to prevent overclaiming. Being precise here builds credibility
rather than costing it.

  REAL AND VERIFIED
  -----------------
    - GCC-PHAT implementation, with band-limiting and sub-sample refinement

  REAL BUT SIMPLIFIED

    - Single straight pipe: no junctions, branches or reflections

    - Any machine learning. The untrained CNN placeholder was removed; the

    - Any leak/non-leak classification. The system assumes what it hears is a

    - FastAPI backend and MQTT telemetry: declared, not wired in.


================================================================================
 PART 12   LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
================================================================================

LIMITATIONS, PLAINLY
--------------------
  1. No classification. The system cannot tell a leak from a truck, a pump or
     a running tap - it assumes a leak and locates it.
  2. Single pipe, no reflections or multipath. Real networks are messier.
  3. Wave velocity is assumed known; position error tracks velocity error 1:1.
  4. Attenuation is not frequency-dependent, though real attenuation is.
  5. GCC-PHAT fails off a cliff, not a slope. Mitigated by the confidence
     gate, not solved.
  6. The confidence threshold is calibrated on synthetic data only.
  7. Two sensors give exactly one solution - no redundancy, no cross-check.
  8. Single-leak assumption.

NEXT STEPS, ROUGHLY IN ORDER OF VALUE
-------------------------------------
  1. Train a real classifier on labelled leak / non-leak recordings and
     reinstate it as Stage 2. archive/ml_model/README.md has the restore steps
     and a training checklist. This is the single biggest gap between prototype
     and product - and note that the archived module gives you working
     plumbing, NOT a working classifier.
  2. Replace strict PHAT with coherence-weighted (Hannan-Thomson) GCC, which
     weights each bin by how much signal it actually contains rather than
     giving all bins equal votes. It degrades far more gracefully than the
     cliff documented in PART 6 - this directly attacks the main weakness.
  3. Extend to three or more sensors for over-determined localisation and
     self-consistency checks.
  4. Wire WNTR for genuine hydraulic modelling driving signal amplitude.
  5. Expose the detector over FastAPI and stream sensor frames over MQTT, so
     it becomes a service rather than a demo.
  6. Calibrate wave velocity in situ - e.g. from a known induced transient -
     rather than assuming it.
